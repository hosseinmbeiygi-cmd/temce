# Impact mapping

Worked transmission chains for the event types that recur. Each gives the mechanism, who gains, who
loses, what to verify, and the trap that makes analysts pick the wrong name.

Use these as a starting structure, not a lookup table. The chain matters more than the conclusion —
if you cannot retrace the steps for the specific company you are naming, you do not have a thesis.

## How to use a chain

Every chain answers the same four questions. Write them out before naming a company:

1. **What changed, and versus what expectation?** The surprise is the tradeable part.
2. **Which P&L line does it hit** — volume, price, input cost, financing cost, or regulatory cost?
3. **Who has that line concentrated?** Share of revenue or costs decides magnitude.
4. **What is already in the price?** Today's move and volume.

The trap common to all of them: reasoning from industry to company. "Airlines are hurt by oil" is
the chain; *which* airline, with what fuel hedging, at what share of cost, is the analysis.

## Interest rates and central bank policy

**Mechanism:** rates set the cost of money, which flows into lender margins, borrower costs, and
the discount rate applied to future earnings.

**Rate cut / dovish surprise**
- Gains: rate-sensitive borrowers — real estate developers, housing finance, autos and consumer
  durables (demand is financed), infrastructure and utilities (leveraged), and long-duration
  growth stocks (lower discount rate lifts distant cash flows most).
- Loses: banks and lenders on net interest margin, insurers on reinvestment yields, and savers'
  proxies.

**Rate hike / hawkish surprise** — invert the above. Banks usually gain on margin in the near term.

**Verify:** what was the consensus expectation? A 25bp cut that was fully priced moves nothing, and
guidance about the *path* often matters more than the decision itself. Read the statement language
and any dot-plot equivalent, not just the headline number.

**Trap:** banks are not a uniform bet. A bank funded by low-cost current accounts gains from
hikes; one dependent on wholesale funding is squeezed. Check the deposit mix before calling it.

## Crude oil

**Mechanism:** oil is simultaneously a revenue line for producers, an input cost for a wide set of
industries, and a macro variable for oil-importing economies.

**Oil up**
- Gains: upstream producers and explorers (direct revenue), oilfield services, and in some markets
  integrated majors.
- Loses: airlines (fuel is commonly 20–35% of operating cost — the cleanest single exposure in
  equities), road and marine logistics, paints and adhesives (crude derivatives are the key input),
  tyres, petrochemicals, and FMCG with packaging-heavy products.
- Macro: oil-importing economies (India, Japan, most of Europe) see currency and inflation
  pressure, which loops back into rates.

**Oil down** — invert. Note that refiners are not a simple inverse: their economics run on the
*crack spread* between crude and product prices, not the crude price alone.

**Verify:** whether the move is supply-driven (OPEC decision, outage, sanctions) or demand-driven
(growth fears). Demand-driven moves carry a recession signal that hurts cyclicals broadly, so the
"winners" list shrinks.

**Trap:** hedging. An airline hedged twelve months out does not feel this quarter's spike, and the
hedge ratio is usually disclosed. Unhedged carriers are the real exposure.

## Currency moves

**Mechanism:** translation of foreign revenue, competitiveness of exports, cost of imported inputs,
and the servicing cost of foreign-currency debt.

**Home currency weakens**
- Gains: exporters and companies earning abroad while costing at home — IT services, pharma
  (generics exporters), auto components, textiles, tourism inbound.
- Loses: importers of inputs (electronics assembly, edible oils, crude-linked), companies with
  unhedged foreign-currency borrowings, and outbound travel.

**Verify:** the revenue split by currency, and the hedging policy. Many exporters hedge 6–12 months
forward, which delays and dampens the benefit — the stock may move on a benefit the P&L will not
show for three quarters.

**Trap:** assuming a weak currency is unambiguously good for an export-heavy index. It also imports
inflation, which pressures rates, which hurts domestic demand names in the same index.

## Tariffs and trade restrictions

**Mechanism:** a tariff is a tax on a specific flow of goods between two specific places. Exposure
is therefore geographic and product-specific, not industry-wide.

- Loses: companies exporting the tariffed product into the tariffing market, in proportion to that
  market's share of their revenue.
- Gains: domestic producers in the tariffing market who compete with the import, and exporters in
  third countries who are not subject to it — the substitution effect is frequently the better and
  less crowded trade.

**Verify:** the exact product codes and the exact countries. Tariff headlines are usually broader
than the measure. Also check the effective date — announced-but-not-in-force measures often get
negotiated away, and the market discounts them accordingly.

**Trap:** naming a big exporter without checking its exposure to *that* market. A company with 4%
of revenue in the tariffing country has a rounding error, not a thesis.

## Industrial metals and commodities

**Mechanism:** input cost for manufacturers, revenue for miners and smelters.

- Metal price up: miners, smelters and integrated producers gain; users lose — autos, construction,
  appliances, capital goods, packaging (aluminium, steel, copper).
- Integrated players with captive mines are insulated from their own input cost, which is why they
  outperform non-integrated peers on a price spike. This is the detail that separates names.

**Verify:** contract structure. Many industrial buyers hold annual or quarterly contracts, so a spot
price spike does not hit the P&L immediately.

## Regulation and government policy

**Mechanism:** changes to what a company may sell, at what price, at what compliance cost.

- Price caps and subsidy cuts hit the regulated sector directly and immediately.
- Compliance mandates hurt the regulated and help the vendors selling compliance — the second-order
  winner is often cleaner because it is less crowded.
- Approvals, licences and spectrum or capacity auctions are binary events with large moves.

**Verify:** the stage. A proposal, a draft rule, a passed law and an in-force rule are four very
different things, and the market prices each differently. A draft that has been discussed for
months is already largely in the price.

**Trap:** treating a government announcement as immediate revenue. Infrastructure and defence
announcements often describe multi-year spending with an uncertain award process; the order book
effect can be years out, while the stock reacts on day one and then fades.

## Defence and geopolitical escalation

**Mechanism:** conflict raises defence budgets, disrupts supply routes, and moves energy and
commodity prices.

- Gains: defence primes and pure-play suppliers, shipbuilders, defence electronics, and — through
  the energy channel — producers.
- Loses: airlines and shipping on route disruption and fuel, tourism, and companies with operations
  or revenue in the affected geography.

**Verify:** whether the country's budget actually funds the implied purchases, and whether the
company sells to that government. Defence procurement is national.

**Trap:** the reflex bid. Defence names spike on headlines and give it back when no order
materialises — this is the category where the "already moved" check matters most.

## Weather, monsoon and agriculture

**Mechanism:** agricultural output drives rural income, which drives rural consumption, and drives
input demand and food inflation.

- Good monsoon or harvest: tractors and farm equipment, agrochemicals and fertilisers, rural-heavy
  FMCG, two-wheelers, rural lenders and microfinance.
- Drought or flood: the above suffer; food inflation rises, which pressures central banks and loops
  into rates.

**Verify:** the forecast versus the long-period average, and regional distribution — an aggregate
"normal" monsoon that is badly distributed hurts specific crops and regions.

**Trap:** this chain is long and slow. Rural demand responds over quarters, not sessions. It is
sound analysis but rarely a next-session catalyst — say so rather than implying immediacy.

## Company events and read-across

**Mechanism:** a single company's news carries information about others.

- **Earnings surprise:** the company moves on the surprise versus consensus, not on whether profit
  grew. Guidance usually matters more than the reported quarter.
- **Read-across to peers:** a supplier's results reveal demand at its customers, and one company's
  margin commentary implies an industry-wide input problem. This is often where the un-priced
  opportunity sits, because coverage focuses on the reporting company.
- **M&A:** the target rallies toward the offer price and stays below it by a spread reflecting deal
  risk; the acquirer commonly falls. Peers of the target re-rate on the implied valuation.
- **Incidents** (plant fire, recall, outage, data breach): the direct hit is the company's; the
  second-order winner is the competitor that picks up the volume, which is usually slower to move.

**Verify:** consensus expectations before calling something a beat or miss, and whether a peer
genuinely shares the exposure or merely the sector label.

**Trap:** "sell the news" on anticipated events. A long-expected approval or a pre-announced deal
can fall on confirmation because the position was already built. Check the run-up before assuming
confirmation is bullish.
